# A broken question costs trust

**Who loses most?** A student with limited preparation time, especially one studying without a teacher nearby. A wrong key can make a correct method feel unreliable; a missing value can consume an entire revision slot. The console prioritizes high-stakes answer and unsound-question defects, combines duplicate reports, and shows a short checkable explanation before technical detail. Student replies explain the outcome respectfully, including when the student was mistaken.

**What would I never auto-apply?** New distractors, diagrams, uncertain subject or syllabus reclassification, or repairs to a question with no valid answer. These require faculty authorship or judgment. I would also hold mathematical answer changes and type migrations for review unless independent, executable evidence establishes their meaning and the schema migration is proven safe. High confidence or two agreeing models alone is insufficient. Here, automatic eligibility is limited to narrow cosmetic transformations, and it still does not mean a change has been saved.

**Week-one metric.** Median time from the first report of a faculty-confirmed defect to a verified saved correction, broken down by severity. Track incorrect applications alongside it as a guardrail, so faster closure cannot hide harmful fixes. This can be measured from report timestamps and persisted audit events without inventing how many students saw a question.

**One next product idea.** When faculty repair a defect, offer a small concept-focused recovery card to students who attempted that question: what changed, the correct method, and one related practice item. Keep learning recovery separate from scoring changes, which need a clear institute policy and an auditable approval path.
